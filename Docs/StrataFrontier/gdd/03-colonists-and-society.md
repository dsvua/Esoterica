# Colonists and Society

## Identity and scale

Every colonist has a stable identity and remains named, inspectable, socially simulated, and directly commandable. The simulation includes:

- Anatomy and health conditions
- Needs and mood
- Skills and aptitudes
- Personality traits and preferences
- Memories, fears, and ambitions
- Relationships and family
- Cultural identity and beliefs
- Personal possessions and roles

Expensive perception, navigation, and activity work may use distance-based LOD, but identity and consequential state remain authoritative.

## Needs and mood

Core needs are hunger, thirst, sleep, thermal comfort, safety, hygiene, recreation, social connection, privacy, and purpose. Shelter contributes to several needs instead of existing as a redundant meter. Spiritual needs depend on personal belief.

Mood is numerically inspectable and arises from concrete memories and current conditions. Avoid need bars that do not create meaningful environmental, scheduling, logistical, or social decisions.

Extreme stress escalates through personality-specific responses such as withdrawal, arguments, refusal, fixation, panic, flight, aggression, or substance use. Severe breaks may override orders temporarily, but should reflect causes rather than arbitrary suicidal behavior. Relationships, environment, support, treatment, and trauma affect recovery.

## Skills, work, and learning

Skills use numerical proficiency plus personal aptitude. Practice, books, instruction, experimentation, tools, health, age, and environment affect learning and performance. Experts teach apprentices. Long-unused skills may become rusty without erasing mastery.

Work is managed through numeric priorities, reusable role templates, schedules, allowed areas, policies, production bills, and direct overrides. Colonists choose eligible work based on priority, urgency, need, skill, distance, equipment, danger, and dependencies.

## Lifecycle and education

The long-term design supports aging, pregnancy, birth, childhood, adulthood, old age, and natural death. Relationships and family decisions are autonomous and consensual. The player shapes housing, healthcare, childcare, education, leave, resources, and broad policy rather than ordering romance or pregnancy.

Children learn through play, chores, school, observation, and apprenticeship. Activities are age appropriate. Culture, caregivers, teachers, experiences, and personality influence skills and beliefs.

New colonists arrive through migration, rescue, refuge, marriage, recruitment, prisoner defection, or other world-grounded circumstances. They physically travel from somewhere and bring relationships, knowledge, possessions, loyalties, and enemies.

## Relationships and culture

Colonists autonomously form friendship, rivalry, romance, family, mentorship, loyalty, jealousy, grief, and grudges. Social change is explainable through compatibility, culture, shared experience, and remembered behavior.

Generated cultural and spiritual traditions affect food, clothing, ritual, work, relationships, burial, leadership, and interpretation of the Echo. Individuals may convert, combine practices, disagree, or contribute to a new local identity. Conflict does not automatically become violence.

Social interactions appear as concise contextual summaries, occasional short speech bubbles, and inspectable memories. Important proposals, disputes, trials, discoveries, and confessions may use richer authored templates. Runtime cloud-generated dialogue is not required.

## Leadership, law, and misconduct

Settlements may develop leaders, offices, councils, appointments, elections, or hereditary structures. The player establishes institutions, laws, and procedures; colonists support, resist, or reinterpret them based on culture, legitimacy, relationships, and results.

Colonists can disobey, steal, assault, sabotage, desert, or commit other misconduct for simulated reasons. Responses include evidence gathering, mediation, restitution, restriction, detention, trial, exile, or release. Torture, slavery, and forced labor are excluded.

## Personal space and property

Settlement production is largely communal, but colonists may own assigned sleeping spaces, clothing, tools, weapons, keepsakes, gifts, money, or inherited objects. Privacy, crowding, noise, theft, beauty, and proximity affect comfort and relationships without requiring management of every trivial utensil.

Rooms are automatically detected and classified. Players configure ownership and allowed use but do not need to name rooms.

## Death and remembrance

Death leaves physical remains and social consequences. Environment and treatment affect decomposition. Cultures define funerals, graves, cremation, preservation, repatriation, and memorials. Death creates grief, inheritance, historical records, faction consequences, and possible Echo effects.

## Faction-wide knowledge

Research unlocks and institutional knowledge belong to the player faction, not to individual colonies or camps. People, books, artifacts, facilities, and fieldwork are still required to acquire knowledge, but the player does not manage transfer of unlocked technologies between their own sites.
